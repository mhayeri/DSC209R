I prefer Plot A, the aisle-by-aisle price comparison. My first draft compared whole categories, so cheap processed aisles like bread sat next to expensive fresh meat, and it wasn't clear whether processing or the kind of food was driving the price. Plot A only compares items inside the same aisle, which takes the kind of food out of the comparison. Within each aisle I split the priced items into thirds by Food Processing Score and plot the median price per 100 kcal of the least and the most processed third. Position on a shared log axis carries the comparison, because prices run from about 10 to 300 cents and a linear axis would crush the cheap aisles together. Sorting by the ratio and drawing a divider lets the reader see the pattern (37 of 47 aisles above the line) before reading any numbers, and the ratio column gives the exact size for anyone who wants it. Orange always marks the less processed side, the same accent the other two plots use. Plot B makes one memorable point, that 56% of products with "organic" in the name are still ultra-processed, but it only shows a share, and within the same aisle organic items score just 0.04 lower, so the story is thinner than it first looks. Plot C is the most striking to look at and shows how little choice most big aisles offer, but its 19,046 dots describe what is on the shelf rather than what it costs a shopper, and the overlap between class and score takes explaining. Plot A has limits too. In heavily processed aisles the "least processed third" is still processed (its median score is 0.87 for muffins and bagels), and price per calorie flatters calorie-dense food. In the final version I'll show how far apart the two thirds are on the processing score and check whether price per gram tells the same story.
