Project 2 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Mustafa Hayeri — muhayeri@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Poorer countries are catching up with richer ones.

Both charts use the same 162 countries: every country with World Bank GDP per capita (constant 2010 US$) in both 1990 and 2019. Countries are split into four quarters of about 40 each by their 1990 income. Each chart ends with the same small inset, the income of the lower three quarters as a share of the richest quarter. The only difference between the two insets is whether China and India are counted.

Each design decision below has a score from -2 (fully deceptive) to +2 (fully earnest).

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Growth rate instead of dollars added (score -1.5). The y-axis is each country's compound annual growth rate. A percentage rewards a low starting point: the poorest quarter's median went from $563 to $1,117 and nearly doubled, while the richest quarter's median added far more dollars and still grew more slowly (a median of 2.3% a year against 1.4%). The whole chart rests on this choice, and it works because growth rates are the standard way economists talk about convergence, so few readers question it. The alternative was dollars added, which is what the AGAINST chart uses.
  • Log scale for 1990 income (score +1). Starting incomes run from about $200 to about $125,000, so a linear axis would pack most countries against the left edge. A log scale is the honest default for income and I would use it whichever side I was arguing. It also gives the poorest countries most of the width, which is where the fastest growers sit, so the eye lands on China and Vietnam first. I considered ranking countries instead of plotting income, but ranks hide how far apart the quarters really are.
  • Population as bubble size and in the inset (score -0.5). Sizing bubbles by population makes China (8.7% a year) and India (4.6%) the two biggest marks on the page, and both sit in the fast-growing corner. The inset weights by population too, which is how the lower three quarters rise from 6.4% to 10.8% of the richest quarter's income. Counting people instead of countries is defensible, since it is usually what "the world got richer" means. What the chart does not say is that China carries most of that rise: leave China out and about three-quarters of the increase disappears. Equal-size dots were the alternative; they made the poor-country cloud look much less impressive.
  • Starting in 1990, with data in both years (score -1). I fixed the window at 1990-2019. Starting in 1970 shrinks the panel to 109 countries and the correlation between starting income and growth to -0.07, which is close to nothing, and within 1990-2000 alone the richer countries actually grew faster. I also tried starting in 2000, where the correlation is stronger (-0.39), but a 19-year window looked like it had been picked for the answer. 1990 reads as a round, neutral date, which is exactly why it works. Requiring data in both years also drops 55 of 217 countries, among them Afghanistan, Somalia, South Sudan, Syria and Yemen, so the poorest quarter is missing several countries hit by war, which are the ones most likely to have stalled. Both charts share this panel, but it helps this side more.
  • Title and trend line (score -1.5). The title states the pattern as a plain fact, and the dashed trend line gives the eye a slope to follow. The correlation behind that line is only -0.25, and 6 of the 41 countries in the poorest quarter are poorer now than in 1990. A careful reader can see that scatter, but the title tells them what to look for first. An earlier draft also had a bold note inside the plot repeating the title; it read as advocacy, so I cut it. I kept Congo, Dem. Rep. labeled at the bottom of the chart, because hiding every counterexample would make the slant easier to spot, not harder.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Dollars added instead of growth rates (score -0.5). Each row shows a quarter's median income in 1990 and 2019 in constant dollars, so the richest quarter's +$13,906 sits next to the poorest quarter's +$554, about 25 times as much. The title only describes what is plotted and lets those two labels make the argument. Earlier titles said "25 times as much" and then listed the two dollar figures, but both read as slanted at first glance. Dollars are what people actually spend, so this is a fair way to ask whether the gap is closing. It is also the measure that automatically favors whoever starts richest, and the chart never mentions that the poorest median nearly doubled while the richest grew by about half. Percent growth was the alternative, and it is what the FOR chart uses.
  • Median country in each quarter (score +0.5). Each quarter is summarized by its median country, so a country of a million people counts the same as China. That keeps one giant from deciding the result, which I think is a reasonable choice for a chart about countries. The cost is that China and India, home to about 2.8 billion people in 2019, barely move the poorest row. A population-weighted mean was the alternative; it would have helped the other side.
  • Linear axis starting at $0 (score +1). The dollar axis is linear and starts at zero, which is the textbook honest choice for amounts of money. Here it also does persuasive work: the poorest quarter's two dots almost touch, so its progress looks like nothing, while the richest quarter's bar spans more than a quarter of the chart. A log axis would have shown the poorer rows moving, so I rejected it for this side.
  • The gap figure in the subtitle (score -0.5). The subtitle gives the gap between the richest and poorest medians: $29,100 in 1990 and $42,500 in 2019. Both numbers are correct, and an absolute gap is a common way to report inequality. But an absolute gap can keep growing even while poorer countries close in proportionally, which is what the FOR chart shows. An earlier draft extrapolated the dollar gains to say the poorest quarter would need over two thousand years to catch up. The arithmetic was right, but it read as a loaded claim, so I replaced it with the plainer gap figure.
  • The inset without China and India (score -1.5). The inset copies the FOR chart's inset, same scale and same colors, with one change: China and India are left out. The share then moves only from 10.3% to 10.7%, so the narrowing in the other chart starts to look like a two-country story. The inset title says what was removed, which is the earnest part. The deceptive part is that I picked those two countries because removing them flattens the bars. China does almost all of the work: on its own it accounts for about three-quarters of the change, and India on its own accounts for none of it.

Final Reflection

Finding two opposing stories was the easy part. Within an hour of exploring the data I had both, and every number on both charts is correct. The hard part was keeping the slant out of sight. My first draft of the AGAINST chart said the poorest countries would need over two thousand years to catch up at their current pace. It was true arithmetic, but it sounded like an argument, so I swapped it for a plain gap figure. Labels were harder than encodings in general: once the measure is chosen, the title only has to describe the chart accurately for the reader to follow it wherever it leads.

Two things surprised me. The first was how much of the hopeful story depends on one country. With everyone counted, the lower three quarters went from 6.4% to 10.8% of the richest quarter's income, and China alone accounts for about three-quarters of that. The second was how fragile the "poorer countries grow faster" pattern is. It holds from 1990 and more strongly from 2000, nearly vanishes from 1970, and reverses within the 1990s. I expected a widely cited finding to be sturdier than that. Using the same inset on both charts was my way of showing this: one exclusion choice turns a doubling into almost nothing.

Before this project I would have said ethical visualization means the numbers are right. I no longer think that is enough, because both of my charts pass that test and still point in opposite directions. My working definition now is that the reader could rebuild the main result, and its strongest counterpoint, from what is on the page, and that the analyst's choices would survive being made before seeing the answer. That gives me a rough bound. A persuasive choice is acceptable if it is disclosed and I would make it no matter which side it helped, like the log axis or a zero-based dollar axis. It becomes misleading when it was picked because it flips the result and the reader has no way to know, like my start year or which countries to drop. Both charts cross that line somewhere, and my scores say where. The line sits in the choices and what gets disclosed, not in any particular chart technique, and I suspect that is why persuasion and deception are so hard to tell apart.
